# Failing the Strategy

"Not sure what the strategy is or how I can contribute to it?"

That’s how most employees respond when you ask them about their company’s strategy.

“Probably something about increasing revenue”, they quickly add, catching my amused look.

And that’s striking. In theory, strategy should guide everyone in the organization. It should create clarity, focus, and alignment.

Instead, it often creates confusion. The leadership team is convinced they have a clear strategy, but employees don’t know how to “execute” it.

The root of this issue lies in a misunderstanding of what strategy actually is.

Leadership teams often assume that when they make decisions, discuss the market, or talk about budgets, they are being “strategic.” After all, it’s about the whole company, and it involves money, right?

But doing strategy isn’t something that just happens. And it’s not about money.

A business strategy is a deliberate set of choices about where to play and how to win (h/t Roger Martin).

It consists of a single goal and the essential steps needed to achieve it.  
And that goal is qualitative, not quantitative.

It’s about creating a “new reality” that the company itself will help bring into existence.

Strategy requires creativity, courage, and the ability to think conceptually. It’s about envisioning a vague path between a dream and the current reality, while being crystal clear about the risks you’re willing to take and the essential steps needed to turn that envisioned goal into reality.

When you create a true strategy, you take deliberate risks—both for your company and your leadership team. It’s about committing to your expectations, assumptions, and hypotheses. It’s exposing yourself to the possibility of success _and_ failure.

The convenience of an unclear strategy is that when things go wrong, you can always blame your Operations or the market. A real strategy, however, can be evaluated on its own merits.

Strategy is the balance between predicting the future and creating it.

And it’s not something you do once a year—it’s something you do every day.

You’ll constantly receive feedback from the market and your employees. When an assumption turns out to be incorrect, or an expectation proves unrealistic, you must adjust your strategy. That’s how your strategy comes to life. It isn’t about creating the perfect strategy and then executing on it. It’s about constantly paying attention and adjusting as you go.

Ultimately, for leadership teams, strategy comes down to one thing: the courage to make real choices. To resist the urge to do everything at once and instead fully commit to what they believe the future could be. And then bring the entire organization along for the journey.

So, the next time you discuss strategy, ask yourself: Is this a story about numbers and market share? Or is it a story of clear ambition, deliberate risks, defined direction, and real choice?
